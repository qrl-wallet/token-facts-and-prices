# QRL to USD: Reading the Price Chart

**Short answer.** A chart plots trades, not value. Before reading a trend into it, check the axis, the time frame, the volume underneath and which venue the data came from. A thin market produces candles that look dramatic and mean very little.

## What the chart plots

A price chart is a record of transactions that happened, drawn against time. Each candle or line segment summarises trades in a window: the open, the high, the low and the close, or a single price sampled at intervals.

That summarisation is already an interpretation. A candle that spans an hour hides the individual trades inside it, and a daily candle hides the whole day. The chart is a compression, and the compression is chosen by whoever built it.

The vertical axis is a second choice. A linear axis makes a move from ten to twenty look the same size as a move from a hundred to a hundred and ten; a logarithmic axis does not. Both are honest, and they answer different questions.

## Volume and thin markets

Volume is the part of the chart most often ignored and most worth reading first. It records how much was traded in each period, and it tells you whether a price move was tested by many participants or by one.

In a thin market a single sale can move the last price several percent, draw a long candle, and imply a trend that the next trade erases. On a chart with no volume panel, that pattern is invisible.

The practical rule: a move on rising volume is a move somebody acted on, and a move on almost no volume is a print. Neither predicts what comes next, and only one of them describes a market where you could actually trade at that price.

## Time frames

The same data looks like three different stories at three zoom levels. A daily chart may show a long decline that a weekly chart shows as a flat stretch, and a five-minute chart may show a pattern that vanishes at hourly resolution.

Short frames are noisier, not more precise. They contain more samples of the same underlying uncertainty, and the patterns visible in them are more likely to be artefacts of the sampling than signals about the asset.

Longer frames hide detail but reduce noise. Choosing one is a decision about what question you are asking: a holder asking about a year and a trader asking about an afternoon need different charts and should not read each other's.

## Comparing venues

Prices are venue-specific. A chart built from one exchange shows that exchange's trades; an aggregator blends several and usually states its method somewhere in the footer.

Two charts of "QRL to USD" from different sources can disagree on the last price and on the shape of the last hour, because they are drawing different trades. That is not a malfunction, and it is a reason to name the source when a chart is used as evidence.

A stablecoin pair adds a small conversion step. A price quoted against USDT is a price in USDT, and turning it into dollars uses a rate that itself moves slightly.

## What a chart cannot tell you

It cannot tell you why a move happened. A price fall in a thin market may reflect a large seller, a venue outage, or a single market order; the chart shows the result and not the cause.

It cannot tell you what will happen next. Patterns have names because people like naming them, not because they predict. A chart that appears to show a shape repeating is a description of the past.

## Reading responsibly

Three habits. Read the volume before the price. Note the time frame and the venue. And treat any pattern as a description rather than a forecast, because the forecast version of a chart is what a signal seller is selling.

One more check is worth making: whether the venue the chart draws from is a market you could actually trade on. A price from an exchange you cannot access is information about that exchange, not about your own options.

If a chart arrives with an arrow drawn on it and a number at the end, the arrow is the product. [Avoid QRL Price Prediction Scams](https://github.com/qrl-wallet/token-facts-and-prices/blob/main/avoid-qrl-price-prediction-scams.md) covers how those pages are built.

For a buyer, the useful question is not where the line goes next but what price you would actually pay, including the spread; [How to Buy QRL Coin](https://github.com/qrl-wallet/token-facts-and-prices/blob/main/how-to-buy-qrl-coin.md) covers that, along with the checks that keep a purchase from going to the wrong address.

## FAQ

### Is there one QRL to USD price?

No. Each venue has its own last trade, and aggregators blend several. The differences are small when volume is high and larger when it is thin.

### Why do two charts disagree?

Different venues, different sampling, different time zones on the axis. A chart from one exchange is not a chart of the whole market.

### Does a pattern on the chart predict the price?

No. A pattern describes what already happened. Treating it as a forecast is a choice, and it is the choice most trading content depends on.

### What should I look at before the price line?

The volume panel and the axis labels, including the time frame. Those two tell you whether the line describes a market or a handful of trades.
